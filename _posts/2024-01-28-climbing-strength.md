In 2023, after four years of lifting, I started rock climbing. It quickly became my main sport, but the transition from lifting to climbing was not straightforward. After months of attempting to excel in both sports simultaneously, I took a step back and **created a training plan that helped me: 1. improve at climbing and 2. maintain my lifts.** I'm sharing my experience because I found no such detailed account when I was looking for a way to structure my training.

First, **you have to choose between being a lifter who climbs or a climber that lifts.** When I was trying to be a lifter and a climber, I lifted 4 days a week, climbed 2, and felt like dogshit 7 days a week. For people that cannot dedicate a huge chunk of their life to sports, trying to lift and climb at the highest possible level is counterproductive. Your body simply cannot recover as fast.

I chose to become a climber that lifts, and took lifting down to once a week, and climbing/training for climbing to 4 days a week. This feels scary, because everyone that lifts is terrified of losing their strength, but **you won't lose as much strength as you think**. With one lifting session per week, I could keep my bench press, overhead press, squats and make small (3%) improvements on my deadlift. Beyond my anecdotical evidence, you can read up on [Greg Nuckols's Guide to Detraining](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/):

> It takes way less effort to maintain muscle and strength than to build additional muscle and strength – it doesn’t take a very large stimulus to maintain your muscle and strength for a very, very long time. For example, in a 2011 study by Bickel and colleagues young lifters (20-35 years old) and older lifters (60-75 years old) initially underwent a 16-week training phase, followed by a 32-week (8-month) phase of training with reduced volume, or complete detraining (24). A third of the lifters stopped training entirely, a third of the lifters reduced their volume by 2/3rds, and a third of the lifters reduced their volume by 8/9ths. **The researchers found that the younger lifters could maintain their muscle and strength over 8 months by maintaining just 1/9th of their original training volume (Figure 10), and older lifters (60-75 years old) could maintain strength with just 1/3rd of their original training volume, though they may still experience some reduction in muscle mass.**

With that said, here is the training plan I've adopted, where you alternate weekly between days "A" and "B":

- Day A: Focus on Squat/Bench Press
	- 4x5 Squat
	- 4x5 Bench Press
	- 3x8 Sumo Deadlift
	- 3x8 Dumbbell Overhead Press
- Day B: Focus on Deadlift/Overhead Press
	- 4x5 Deadlift
	- 4x5 Overhead Press
	- 3x8 Front Squat
	- 3x8 Incline Bench Press

We see that both days contain some variation of all the main lifts, and only include two heavyweight 4x5 exercises (as they are very physically and mentally taxing). They take roughly one hour to complete.

This fits within my weekly training schedule:

- Sunday: Rest
- Monday: Performance climbing
- Tuesday: Submaximal climbing + Core exercises + Antagonist/Stabilizer exercises
- Wednesday: Submaximal climbing + Hangboarding
- Thursday: Rest
- Friday: Lifting
- Saturday: Submaximal climbing + Climbing-specific exercises
